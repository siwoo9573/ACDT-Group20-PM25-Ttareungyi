Does bad air keep Seoul off its bikes?

Our hypothesis: on days with higher PM2.5, fewer people rent Ttareungyi bikes. H₁: mean daily rides on Good days > on Bad days.

Every light on this map of Seoul is a Ttareungyi station, raised by how often it was used. The blue ribbon is the Han River. 80,803,663 rides, 727 days, 2,904 stations. The haze is the question.

On bad-air days, Seoul rides about 10% less.

Compared with every other day in two years, days rated Bad for PM2.5 had 10.5% fewer rentals. Good days averaged 108,623 rides, Bad days 96,359 (one-sided t-test, p = 0.015).

Clearing the haze

Remove what moves with the air, one layer at a time

Cold, rain, wind, the season and the day of the week all travel with bad air. Each step takes one layer away. The haze over the city thins by how much of the day-to-day swing in rides we can now explain.

How to read it

Each estimate is the change in daily rides with a 95% confidence interval. If the interval crosses zero, the data cannot tell the effect from no effect.

Step 1. Raw comparison

Bad-air days against all other days: −10.5%

The interval runs from −21% to +1.6%. Once we allow for today looking a lot like yesterday, even the raw gap is shaky (p = 0.09).

Step 2. Same month only

Compare bad-air days only with other days in the same month: +19.4%

The sign flips. Within a month, bad-air days are not the quiet days. The raw gap came from which months the bad air fell in.

Step 3. Weather and calendar

Add temperature, rain, snow, wind, humidity, weekdays and holidays: +3.2%

The interval now includes zero, and our controls explain 83% of the day-to-day swing in rides.

Step 4. Machine learning controls

Let random forests model weather and season as flexibly as the data allows (Double Machine Learning): +1.1%

For PM2.5 itself the answer is −0.3% per 10 µg/m³. Any effect larger than about ±2% is ruled out.

The leisure test

Maybe only leisure riders stay home

Commuters may ride whatever the air. People cycling for fun can skip a grey day. We tracked two signals of leisure riding, with every rule fixed before we looked at any result. The glowing ribbon behind this text is the Han River: 159 stations within 500 m of the water.

159 stations

Within 500 m of the Han River

The riverside parks are Seoul's leisure route. These stations carry 6.0% of rides on an average day: 5.4% on workdays and 7.4% on days off. We checked a 1 km cutoff as well, and walked the 39 borderline stations on a street map: 7 easy to reach, 24 with a detour, 1 blocked, 7 unclear.

7.0% of rides

Round trips

Returned to the station they left from, after more than 2 minutes. Shorter same-station returns are likely bike swaps. Round trips are 6.0% of rides on workdays and 9.0% on days off.

Days off show no clear response. Workdays show a small one.

On workdays the Han River share of rides dips about 1.2% per 10 µg/m³ (not pre-specified; a relative-share change only), from 5.37% to about 5.31%. It holds in every robustness check, but Han River ride counts themselves do not change significantly, and days off and workdays do not differ (p = 0.53 to 0.79). Round trips show nothing.

What it means

Myth and reality

Pick a belief about fine dust and cycling. See what our data says about it, and how sure we are.

Who rides, and how far, follows the calendar

Raw comparisons with no controls, so read them as patterns. Good to Bad air barely moves anything. Workday to day off moves almost everything.

If bad air did push riders into cars

Our data shows no ride loss to convert, so this is a what-if. Choose how many bike kilometres disappear and how many of them become one-person car trips.

For scale, Ttareungyi riders covered about 259,000 km on an average day in 2024 and 2025. The default, 5,200 km, is 2% of that: the largest daily loss our confidence intervals still allow. Sua's scenario table uses 1,000 km.

What we recommend

Plan bikes around weather, not dust

Temperature and rain carry about 80% of what our models use to predict daily rides. Daily PM2.5 readings add little to prediction.

Don't read raw counts as pollution damage

The 10% gap on bad-air days is a seasonal pattern. Citing it as proof that fine dust deters cycling points policy at the wrong cause.

Measure at the hour, keep green options open

Hourly station data and alert timing are needed to see commute-hour reactions. Meanwhile, keeping transit and bikes attractive on bad-air days guards against the car scenario above.

How we know

Three ways of drawing the same system

Each map fixes a blind spot in the one before.

How we kept ourselves honest

  • The Han River cutoffs (500 m main, 1 km check), the round-trip rule and the robustness checks were written down before we saw any result.
  • Blank rainfall cells follow the weather agency's rule. Coding blanks as no rain reproduces its official count of 227 rain days exactly.
  • We dropped 28 February 2025: the source file stops at Excel's row limit, so that day is undercounted. Three days with no wind data were dropped too.
  • The single Very Bad day (PM2.5 above 75 µg/m³) is counted as Bad, a rule fixed before the analysis.
  • Standard errors allow for autocorrelation (Newey-West). DML uses 5-fold cross-fitting, repeated over 5 seeds.
  • Results that went against our hypothesis are reported as clearly as the ones that went for it.

Limits of what we can say

  • Only 58 Bad days in two years, so Bad-day estimates have wide intervals. Continuous PM2.5 estimates are much tighter.
  • Daily city totals hide commute-hour spikes and station-level choices.
  • Alerts were in force on only 7 days, all for PM10, so reactions to alerts could not be tested.
  • Han River events and park closures are not in our data and could move the riverside share.
  • We cannot see what people did instead of riding, so any emissions figure is a scenario.

What we learned about deciding with data

  • A significant raw gap is the start of a question. The first control we added flipped its sign.
  • A result that changes with one control, as ours did with humidity, is not a finding yet.
  • "No effect" needs a size. Ruling out effects beyond about ±2% says more than "not significant".

What we would study next

  • Hourly rentals against hourly PM2.5, to test commute-hour reactions.
  • Forecast and alert timing, to test whether riders respond to warnings rather than measured air.
  • Transit card data on bad-air days, to see where riders go instead.
